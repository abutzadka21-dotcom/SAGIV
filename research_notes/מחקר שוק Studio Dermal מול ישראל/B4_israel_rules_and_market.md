## Q1. Misleading advertising: Consumer Protection Law 1981 (sections 2 and 7), covert advertising, enforcement

### Takeaway
Any fact an ad states about the product's properties must be provable on demand. If the advertiser cannot satisfy the Commissioner with evidence, that alone is prima facie proof the ad misled. Both the advertiser and whoever brought the ad to publication are liable. An ad dressed as an article or news item without a clear "ad" label is deemed misleading. Fines from the Authority have historically been rare (about five a year, according to one critical article). So the practical risk for an online seller is more likely class actions and ad rejection by the platforms [INFERENCE].

### Inferences
- [INFERENCE] For an instant eye tightener, every number or performance claim needs a substantiation file in hand before the ad runs. Examples: "results in 2 minutes", "lasts up to X hours", "X% less puffiness", "clinically tested". Once the Commissioner asks, the burden of proof is on the advertiser.
- [INFERENCE] Two common direct-to-consumer formats match the covert-ad rule unless clearly labelled as advertising (for example "פרסומת"): "advertorial" landing pages styled as news or reviews, and UGC-style creatives presented as independent opinion.
- [INFERENCE] Liability covers "whoever brought the ad to publication", which may include an Israeli agency, media buyer or influencer. An Israeli seller cannot shift liability onto the foreign brand by copying its claims.
- [INFERENCE] Before/after demos are factual claims about results. A real, unedited and representative demo is defensible if documented. A filtered, edited, cherry-picked or lighting-manipulated demo is misleading under s.2. Platform policy is a separate, stricter layer (see Q11).
- [INFERENCE] Class actions are the main private-enforcement route (the Shein example above). An e-commerce launch that relies on aggressive claims and price anchoring is an attractive class-action target.

## Q2. "Price before discount" (מחיר לפני הנחה), reference prices and fake sales

### Takeaway
The search results show no enacted statutory formula for a reference price. Two proposals exist:
- **2017 government bill** ("Amendment No. 54, Special Sale"): a sale only after 30 days at full price, a cap of 45 sale days a year, and a "before" price equal to the lowest regular price in the previous 3 months.
- **2022 memorandum:** again, the lowest price charged in the 3 months before the sale.

I could not confirm whether either became law by October 2026. Fake "was" prices can already be challenged today as misleading under the general ban.

## Q3. Fake urgency, countdown timers, fake low stock, subscription traps (דפוסים אפלים, dark patterns)

### Takeaway
The Israeli Authority has engaged with dark patterns in two ways:
- An OECD-related Hebrew document dated 21/09/2023.
- An international review with 27 enforcement authorities from different countries, which found that 75.7% of websites and apps used at least one dark pattern.

Israeli coverage lists "almost sold out!" messages as pressure tactics, and names auto-renewal traps as a frequent finding. I found no binding Israeli dark-patterns rule and no guidance on countdown timers. False urgency or scarcity would be challenged as misleading under s.2 [INFERENCE].

### Inferences
- [INFERENCE] Each of these is a false statement about price or availability: a countdown timer that restarts on page reload, an "only 3 left" counter not tied to real stock, or "sale ends tonight" that never ends.
