## KQ1. Meta: before/after, personal attributes, negative self-perception, unrealistic outcomes, health and wellness

### Takeaway
Meta's current Health and Wellness standard treats cosmetic products as an age-restricted category. Ads "must be targeted to people at least 18 years or older" and must not contain "statements of inferiority about physical appearance". According to the tool's summary of that page, cosmetic before/after transformations are restricted content (18+), not banned. A 2026 secondary source says Meta rewrote the policy in July 2026 and quotes the same phrase. An older Meta help page still says before/after may not be used "to display idealized results", so expect inconsistent review. Separately, Meta bans copy that asserts or implies personal attributes (including age and physical health), ads that "may contribute to a negative self-perception" for health products, and "promises or suggestions of unrealistic outcomes for health". These standards are global, so they bind ads served in Israel (scope is GK).

- [INFERENCE] If the primary-page wording holds, a half-face demo for an eye-tightening cosmetic sits in Meta's 18+ restricted bucket, not a banned one. Because two Meta texts conflict, expect uneven review: keep a non-before/after variant ready and be prepared to appeal.
- [INFERENCE] Second-person body or age copy ("your eye bags", "over 50?", Hebrew "יש לך שקיות?") breaks two Meta rules at once: personal attributes (age, physical health) and statements of inferiority. Product-centric copy ("This formula temporarily tightens the look of under-eye skin") follows Meta's own pattern ("Find black singles today.").
- [INFERENCE] A specific result in a set time ("bags gone in 2 minutes") needs qualifiers ("temporarily", "the look of", "results vary"): the 2026 secondary source says such claims without qualifiers still get rejected as clickbait, and the unrealistic-outcomes rule applies on top.
- [INFERENCE] No Meta text returned addresses the "Concealer Is A Scam" listicle as such. Its risk on Meta comes from the deceptive-practices standard ("exaggerated claims", celebrity bait) and from the lander matching the ad.
- [GENERAL KNOWLEDGE, high] Meta's Advertising Standards apply to all ads on Meta technologies worldwide, so they bind ads targeted to Israel. Not verified this round.

## KQ2. TikTok: cosmetics, before/after, exaggerated or misleading claims (and Israel)

### Takeaway
TikTok Ads is stricter than Meta on demonstrations. Its "Misleading and false content" policy (tool summary) does not allow "product effect comparisons, such as before-and-after results, which may cause viewers to have a false or distorted impression". It also bans promising or exaggerating a product's effect and "absolute terms about a product in relation to time" (example: "Get slim legs right away"). TikTok Shop guidance names "demonstrating instant removal of eye bags or similar results after one use when exaggerated or unrealistic" as a violation and requires a "results may vary" disclaimer on before/after. Israel is an available TikTok Ads market with Hebrew support.

## KQ3. Google Ads: misrepresentation, unrealistic claims, before/after for cosmetics

### Takeaway
Google's Misrepresentation policy bans claims that "entice the user with an improbable result (even if this result is possible) as the likely outcome". Its Clickbait policy bans "secrets, scandals or other sensationalist" hooks and "clearly altered zoomed in body parts". Per the tool, Google does not allow "before and after" images "to promote significant alterations to the human body". Suspension needs a warning at least seven days ahead.

### Cited Findings
- [VERIFIED] Tool text: "Google Ads prohibits making inaccurate claims or claims that entice the user with an improbable result (even if this result is possible) as the likely outcome a user can expect." Result set: [Misrepresentation: Unreliable claims](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/15936857?hl=en-IE); [Misrepresentation](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/6020955?hl=en); [False, misleading, or unrealistic claims](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/6086777?hl=en).
- [VERIFIED] Tool text: "You must not use false or unrealistic claims to entice customers, especially when suggesting results that are improbable or cannot be guaranteed. This applies even if such results are technically possible but not typical." Same result set.
- [VERIFIED] Tool text, example: "Extreme weight loss products or programmes, such as a weight loss ad that says you can eat whatever you want and lose 10 pounds in a month". Same result set.
- [VERIFIED] Tool text: "Violations of this policy will not lead to immediate account suspension without prior warning. A warning will be issued at least seven days prior to any suspension of your account." Same result set.
- [VERIFIED] Tool text: "Google Ads does not allow ads that use "before and after" images to promote significant alterations to the human body. Additionally, on YouTube and Discover Feed, ads containing false or exaggerated claims related to curing diseases or other health conditions cannot show misleading "before-and-after" images." Result set: [Clickbait ads](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/15936667?hl=en); [YouTube and Discover Feed ad requirements](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/10249050?hl=en).
- [VERIFIED] Tool text: "Ads that use clickbait tactics or sensationalist text or imagery to drive traffic are not allowed. - [INFERENCE] Whether a temporary under-eye tightening counts as a "significant alteration to the human body" is unclear. The conservative reading: no before/after imagery in Google ads (YouTube, Display, Demand Gen).
- [GENERAL KNOWLEDGE, high] Google Ads policies are global, so they apply to ads served in Israel.

## KQ4. UK ASA/CAP: cosmetics claims, "instant" eye claims, before/after, filters, Botox comparisons, perception studies

### Takeaway
The ASA treats before/after images as objective claims. The "after" must be representative, and the advertiser must hold signed and dated proof that the photos are unaltered. Retouching eye-area lines in an eye-cream ad is unacceptable. Filters that exaggerate a beauty product's effect are misleading (Feb 2021, Skinny Tan). "Instant" eye claims depend on product-specific evidence:
- the ASA ruled against The Perfect Cosmetics Company (19 Dec 2018) over "dramatic results instantly" with before/after photos;
- the same brand reports that a later ASA case accepted clinical-trial evidence for its My Perfect Eyes instant, temporary eye claim (self-reported; ASA text not returned).
Consumer-perception percentages work only as perception claims with adequate samples: Beauty Pie's "92%" from "28 participants" was questioned. "Better than Botox!" was ruled misleading and a medicinal claim.

### Cited Findings
**Before/after and production techniques**
- [VERIFIED] Tool text: "If you're planning to use before-and-after photos in your marketing, you must hold signed and dated proof that the photos are genuine and haven't been digitally altered." Result set, most likely CAP AdviceOnline [Before and after photos](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/before-and-after-photos.html).
- [VERIFIED] Tool text, two exact fragments of one sentence: "'Before and After' images are treated as objective claims just the same as written claims" and "any 'after' image should be representative of what consumers can generally obtain from the product." Same result set. (The tool's next sentence on comparable conditions began "This means": the tool's own inference.)
- [VERIFIED] Tool text: "Re-touching related to any characteristics directly relevant to the apparent performance of the product being advertised is unacceptable. For example, removing or reducing the appearance of lines and wrinkles around the eyes for an eye cream advertisement or increasing the length or thickness of eyelashes in an advertisement for mascara." Result set, most likely [Beauty and Cosmetics: The use of production techniques](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/cosmetics-the-use-of-production-techniques.html) and [Cosmetic production techniques Advertising Guidance (PDF)](https://www.asa.org.uk/static/uploaded/ac36e7f4-d2e2-44df-8e70c3719566fb3e.pdf).

## KQ7. US FDA: cosmetic vs drug line, and MoCRA

### Takeaway
The FDA decides by intended use, and claims in ads and online establish it. Making lines "less noticeable" by moisturizing, or makeup and "primers" that hide signs of ageing, are cosmetic uses. Removing wrinkles or increasing collagen makes a product a drug or device. MoCRA requires facility registration (renewed every two years), product listing (updated annually) and reports of serious adverse events within 15 business days. The small-business exemption (under $1,000,000 average annual sales) does not apply to products that "regularly come in contact with mucus membrane of the eye".

### Cited Findings
- [VERIFIED] Tool text: "Whether a product is a cosmetic or a drug under the law is determined by a product's intended use." ## KQ8. Fake urgency and price anchoring: UK and US enforcement

### Takeaway
UK: in the CMA's Emma Sleep case the High Court confirmed on 22 May 2026 that misleading countdown timers (restarting on refresh; offers continuing after the deadline), false "High Demand" messages and discount claims broke consumer law; Emma Sleep gave undertakings. On 30 July 2026 the court found specific "Was £X, NOW £Y" breaches but rejected the CMA's fixed 1:2 sales-volume test for reference prices: whether a reference price is genuine is "multi-factorial". US: under 16 CFR 233.1 a former price must be a bona fide price offered "for a reasonably substantial period of time", and an inflated price set up to advertise a big reduction makes the bargain "a false one". Secondary sources say the FTC's 2022 dark-patterns staff report named fake countdown timers. No US enforcement case on timers was returned.

### Inferences
- [INFERENCE] Studio Dermal's "$79.99 compare-at" against $29.99 is lawful only if $79.99 is a genuine price. In the US: a bona fide price at which the product was openly offered for a reasonably substantial period. In the UK: a genuine reference price on a multi-factorial test. If the product never sold at $79.99, it is a textbook "false" bargain in the US and a "Was/Now" breach risk in the UK. If "compare at" means other sellers' prices, different US sections apply (233.2/233.3, not verified).
- [INFERENCE] Timers that reset on refresh, or offers that continue after the clock hits zero, are the specific conduct found unlawful in Emma Sleep. They are also a platform risk: TikTok's price-mismatch rule and Meta's deceptive-practices standard apply in Israel.
- Israeli consumer-protection law on these practices is out of scope (Israel stream).

- No FTC enforcement action against a fake timer or stock counter was returned. 16 CFR 233.2-233.3 not returned. DMCC Schedule 20 wording on false limited-time offers not returned. Other CMA urgency cases (e.g. Wowcher) not searched.
