## KQ1. Timeline of claims in the category, and evidence of skepticism

### Takeaway
In the US and UK the "instant under-eye fix" promise is decades old. It runs from a drugstore "3-minute" serum (Sudden Change, sold for 20+ years) and the Preparation H hack (online by April 2008), through "risk-free trial" infomercial skincare (Hydroxatone, class action 2011-2013) and MLM viral demo videos (Jeunesse Instantly Ageless, framed as "Botox in a bottle" and "Facelift in a bottle?!", wave around 2015-2017), to paid local-TV "10-Minute Challenge" segments (Plexaderm, 2019-2021). Prestige versions followed (Peter Thomas Roth Instant FIRMx Eye, TikTok-viral in 2021, later an "upgraded, sheer-tinted" version), and then a 2025-2026 wave of DTC clones on Meta and Taboola. Skepticism is documented: residue, short duration, "liquid glass" and high pH, TV consumer "tests", and lawsuits over trial subscriptions. It targets durability, residue and seller honesty more than the visible effect itself, which the market accepts as real but temporary.

### Background: [GENERAL KNOWLEDGE], not verified this session

**ASA/CAP system**
- [GK, high] The ASA applies the CAP Code (non-broadcast) and BCAP Code (broadcast). Since March 2011 the remit covers marketers' claims on their own websites and social media as well as paid ads, including influencer posts and paid social ads (Meta, TikTok) and native ads (Taboola) seen in the UK.
- [GK, medium-high on rule numbers] Core CAP rules:
  - 3.1: no materially misleading ads;
  - 3.7: documentary evidence must be held before publication for objective claims;
  - exaggeration and testimonial rules in Section 3 (testimonials must be genuine and cannot make claims that would otherwise be misleading);
  - Section 12 (medicines, medical devices, health-related products and beauty products): 12.1 requires objective claims to be backed by evidence, where appropriate consisting of trials on people.
- [GK, medium] ASA approach to cosmetic efficacy:
  - it expects evidence on the finished product (not only ingredient-supplier data);
  - in-use studies on people for perceptible effects;
  - claims worded no stronger than the evidence.
  - "Reduces the appearance of" claims are treated as appearance claims. "Instant facelift", "eliminates bags" or claims implying permanence or dramatic change are judged on the overall impression, including imagery.
- [GK, high] Pre-production techniques: in July 2011 the ASA banned two L'Oréal ads, Lancôme Teint Miracle (Julia Roberts) and Maybelline The Eraser (Christy Turlington), because retouching exaggerated what the products could achieve. The complaints came from MP Jo Swinson. Principle since then: retouching that exaggerates a cosmetic's effect is misleading, and small-print disclaimers cannot cure a misleading main impression.
- [GK, medium-high] Filters (2021): in February 2021 the ASA upheld complaints about Instagram Stories in which influencers promoted tanning products for Skinnydip London and Sheikhbeauty using beauty filters, which exaggerated the products' efficacy. The ASA then publicly said filters should not be applied to images in ads for beauty and cosmetic products if they are likely to exaggerate the effect the product can achieve. The context was influencer Sasha Pallari's #filterdrop campaign. This is a paraphrase, not a quote.
- [GK, medium] Before/after: not banned by the ASA as such. To be acceptable it must:
  - show genuine, typical results of the product alone;
  - be shot under the same conditions (light, angle, make-up, expression, distance) with no retouching or filters;
  - disclose timing.
  It must not imply permanence when the effect is temporary.
- [GK, medium] Botox and procedure comparisons: Botox is a prescription-only medicine (POM). POMs may not be advertised to the public (CAP rule 12.12; Human Medicines Regulations 2012), and ASA/CAP ran a social-media enforcement project against Botox ads around 2020-2021. A cosmetic claiming to work "like Botox" or as a "non-surgical facelift" needs evidence of comparable results, invites comparison with a POM or procedure, and is high-risk. Specific ASA rulings on "Botox in a bottle" cosmetics were NOT verified.
- [GAP] No ASA ruling on eye tighteners, Plexaderm, Instantly Ageless, PTR FIRMx or Sudden Change was verified. The searches that would have checked this were refused.

**Cosmetics claims law (substantiation)**
- [GK, high-medium] Great Britain retains Cosmetic Products Regulation (EC) 1223/2009 as amended for GB (Schedule 34 to the Product Safety and Metrology etc. (Amendment etc.) (EU Exit) Regulations 2019). Key elements:
  - safety assessment, a UK Responsible Person, and notification via the UK SCPN portal;
  - Article 20 on claims: labelling, presentation and advertising must not imply characteristics or functions the product lacks.
  OPSS is the policy lead; enforcement is mainly by local Trading Standards. Northern Ireland follows the EU Regulation under the Windsor Framework.
- [GK, high] The common criteria for cosmetic claims (retained Commission Regulation (EU) No 655/2013) are: legal compliance, truthfulness, evidential support, honesty, fairness, informed decision-making. In paraphrase:
  - claims need adequate, verifiable evidence using sound methodology suited to the claim;
  - the presentation of performance must not go beyond the evidence;
  - claims must be clear to the average end user;
  - claims must not denigrate competitors or legally used ingredients.
- [GK, medium] Supporting guidance:
  - the EU Technical Document on Cosmetic Claims (Annex III "free from" and Annex IV "hypoallergenic" applied from July 2019; widely used in GB as best practice);
  - Cosmetics Europe guidelines on efficacy evaluation;
  - CTPA guidance (UK trade association).
- [GK, medium] Cosmetic vs medicine borderline (MHRA): a product presented as treating or preventing disease, or as restoring, correcting or modifying physiological functions, can be a medicine. For eye products the higher-risk wording is "treats eye bags", "reduces swelling/inflammation", "drains fluid" and "repairs collagen". "Temporarily reduces the appearance of puffiness" is cosmetic wording.

**CMA and the DMCC Act 2024**
- [GK, high] The DMCC Act received Royal Assent on 24 May 2024. From 6 April 2025 a new unfair commercial practices regime (replacing the CPRs 2008) and CMA direct enforcement apply: the CMA can find breaches and fine up to 10% of global turnover (individuals: up to GBP 300,000) without a court.
- [GK, medium-high] Banned practices (Schedule 20) include:
  - the former CPR blacklist, for example:
    - falsely stating that a product is available only for a very limited time to force an immediate decision;
    - paid editorial content ("advertorial") not clearly identified as paid;
    - falsely representing oneself as a consumer;
  - new fake-review practices:
    - submitting, commissioning or incentivising fake reviews;
    - incentivised reviews not clearly disclosed;
    - presenting reviews in a misleading way (e.g., suppressing negative reviews).
  Traders who publish reviews must take reasonable and proportionate steps to prevent and remove fake or misleading ones. The CMA published fake-review guidance with its Unfair Commercial Practices guidance (CMA207) in April 2025.
- [GK, medium-high] Drip pricing: an invitation to purchase must show the total price, including unavoidable fees and charges. Mandatory fees revealed later in checkout are misleading. Genuinely optional extras are treated differently.
- [GK, medium] Fake urgency and false scarcity (countdown timers, "only 3 left", "price ends tonight") must be true. The CMA's online choice architecture work and its cases against online sellers over pressure selling and countdown clocks (e.g., Emma Sleep, Wowcher) signal this is an enforcement priority.
- [GK, medium on content; low on date] Subscription contracts regime (Part 4, Chapter 2):

  - easy exit (cancellation as easy as sign-up);

- [GK, high] Hidden advertising: the ASA and CMA both require paid influencer and affiliate content to be obviously identifiable (e.g., "#ad" upfront).

## KQ4. US regulation: FTC (anti-ageing claims, fake reviews, negative option), FDA cosmetic vs drug line, MoCRA

### Takeaway

### Background: [GENERAL KNOWLEDGE], not verified this session

**FTC**
- [GK, high] The FTC Act covers unfair or deceptive practices (Section 5) and false advertising of foods, drugs, devices, services or cosmetics (Section 12). Objective claims need a reasonable basis before dissemination. Establishment claims ("clinically proven", "80% reduction") need the level of proof they assert.
- [GK, high on the guidance; medium on application] FTC Health Products Compliance Guidance (Dec 2022) sets a "competent and reliable scientific evidence" standard for health-related claims. Pure appearance claims for cosmetics are usually judged on reasonable basis; claims of physiological effect move toward the health-claim bar.
- [GK, high] L'Oréal USA consent order (June 2014): the FTC charged that ads for Lancôme Génifique and L'Oréal Paris Youth Code claimed, without support, that the products targeted users' genes to produce younger-looking skin, including "clinically proven" claims. The order bars such unsupported claims.
- [GK, medium on names and years] FTC "free trial" skincare cases include:
  - FTC v. Bunzai Media Group (2015, AuraVie skincare);
  - FTC v. Tarr Inc. (2017);
  - FTC v. RevMountain (2017);
  - FTC v. Apex Capital Group (2018).
  These were online anti-ageing creams and supplements sold through "free trial" offers with hidden recurring billing. Together with Hydroxatone (verified), they are the US trust-erosion backdrop for DTC anti-ageing offers.
- [GK, high] Endorsement Guides (16 CFR Part 255), revised June 2023:
  - endorsements must reflect honest opinions and actual experience;
  - material connections (paid creators, free product, affiliates) must be disclosed clearly and conspicuously;
  - since 2009, "results not typical" disclaimers give no safe harbor, so testimonials showing atypical results need a clear disclosure of generally expected results.
- [GK, high on scope and dates; medium on amount] FTC Rule on the Use of Consumer Reviews and Testimonials (16 CFR Part 465), announced 14 Aug 2024 and effective 21 Oct 2024. It prohibits:
  - fake or false consumer reviews and testimonials, including AI-generated ones and misattributed celebrity testimonials;
  - buying reviews conditioned on sentiment;
  - undisclosed insider reviews;
  - company-controlled "independent" review sites;
  - review suppression through threats or false accusations;
  - buying or selling fake social media indicators (followers, views).
  Knowing violations carry civil penalties (about USD 51,744 per violation in 2024, inflation-adjusted yearly).
- [GK, high] Negative option:
  - The FTC's "Click-to-Cancel" amendments to the Negative Option Rule (finalized 16 Oct 2024) were vacated by the US Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit on 8 July 2025, because the FTC skipped a required preliminary regulatory analysis.
  - ROSCA (2010) still applies to online negative-option sales: clear disclosure of material terms before billing information is taken, express informed consent, and a simple cancellation mechanism.
  - The FTC kept enforcing ROSCA, e.g., the Amazon Prime case settled 25 Sep 2025 for USD 2.5 billion (USD 1 billion civil penalty plus USD 1.5 billion refunds).
  - Whether the FTC restarted negative-option rulemaking by October 2026: NOT verified.
- [GK, medium] State auto-renewal laws add obligations. California's amended Automatic Renewal Law (effective 1 July 2025) requires express consent and online click-to-cancel, among other things; several other states have similar laws.
- [GK, medium-high] The FTC Impersonation Rule (effective April 2024) bans impersonating government or businesses, e.g., falsely implying affiliation. This is relevant to fake "As seen on" media logos and news-site lookalike advertorial landers.
- [GK, high on the role] Self-regulation: BBB National Programs' National Advertising Division (NAD) routinely reviews cosmetic efficacy claims, often on competitor challenge. Eye-tightener NAD cases were NOT verified.

**FDA: cosmetic vs drug**
- [GK, high; paraphrase of FD&C Act 201(i) and 201(g)(1)]
  - A cosmetic is an article intended to be applied to the body for cleansing, beautifying, promoting attractiveness or altering the appearance.
  - A drug is an article intended to diagnose, cure, mitigate, treat or prevent disease, or to affect the structure or any function of the body.
  - Intended use is shown by claims (labels, ads, websites, social media, including testimonials a company adopts), by consumer perception, and by ingredients with well-known therapeutic uses.
- [GK, high on the principle; medium on letter details] The anti-ageing line:
  - Drug claims include: removes wrinkles, increases collagen, repairs or rebuilds skin, boosts cell or gene activity, relaxes muscles (Botox-like), reduces swelling or inflammation.
  - FDA warning letters to skincare marketers (e.g., Lancôme USA, 2012) cited claims of this kind.
  - Cosmetic claims include: "temporarily reduces the appearance of fine lines", "visibly tightens the look of under-eye skin", "reduces the look of puffiness".
- [GK, high on core; medium on exemption details] MoCRA (Modernization of Cosmetics Regulation Act of 2022), signed 29 Dec 2022:
  - facility registration and product listing (FDA enforcement from 1 July 2024);
  - serious adverse event reports to FDA within 15 business days;
  - safety substantiation by the "responsible person";
  - label contact information for adverse-event reports (by 29 Dec 2024);
  - recordkeeping and mandatory recall authority.
  - Rules on GMP, fragrance-allergen labeling and talc testing were required but, at my cutoff, not final.
  - Small businesses (under about USD 1 million average annual sales) are exempt from registration/listing and GMP, except for certain products, including those that regularly contact the mucous membrane of the eye or alter appearance for more than 24 hours.
  - Status of the GMP, allergen and talc rules at October 2026: NOT verified.

Avoid "Botox", "lift muscles", "collagen", "permanent" and "removes bags".

## KQ6. Positioning strategies that worked in similarly sophisticated beauty markets

### Takeaway
Within this category the verified winners moved from bigger claims to:
- mechanism stories ("Botox in a bottle" peptides; "shale clay invisible shield");
- proof rituals ("10-Minute Challenge"; fan-dried live demo);

### Inferences (positioning options for the Israeli seller; all [INFERENCE])
1. **Honest-temporary identity (Level 5 identification).** For example "The 10-minute fix for the days that matter", tied to occasions: events, photos, video calls, weddings. Say "temporary" first, turning the category's weak point into a credibility signal. This targets disillusioned buyers who distrust "miracle" claims.
2. **Mechanism refinement (Level 4).** Position against the known failures of older film-formers: no white cast, no flaking or cracking, flexible film, wearable under make-up, gentle on the eye area. Only use these if supported by tests. A comparative "not liquid glass" or "silicate-free" angle is possible only if true. Comparative claims must be verifiable and fair: UK comparative-advertising rules, US Lanham Act exposure, and Meta's misleading-claims rules.
3. **Needle-free identity without naming Botox.** "For women who don't do needles" (the Plexaderm-style deposition) carries far less risk than "Botox in a bottle".
4. **Proof ritual, made compliant.** A single-take, timestamped, unfiltered application video (a "watch it set" ritual). On Meta, avoid split-screen before/after frames.
5. **Trust-first offer as deposition of the clone wave.** One-time purchase, visible total price, real reviews and a clear returns policy, contrasted (implicitly) with low-rated DTC sellers (Studio Dermal Trustpilot 2.3, brief).
